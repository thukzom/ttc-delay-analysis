The bus isn’t late. You are waiting.

I spent four years getting around Toronto on the TTC, and the part I never got used to was not the delay itself. It was standing at a stop in February with no idea whether the bus was two minutes away or twenty. So I went looking for an answer in the City’s own data — not how late the buses were, but how long people actually stood there waiting, and what would have to change to make that number smaller.

The measure that matters is the gap, not the delay

The data records two different things, and I think most people reading it pick the wrong one. Min Delay is how late a vehicle was. Min Gap is how long the hole in service lasted. The first is what an operations team is measured on; the second is what you experience on the sidewalk. On this data an average delay of 14 minutes leaves an average gap of 23.

Waiting is worse than lateness, and not by a fixed amount

During a gap of G minutes you wait G/2 on average instead of the H/2 you would have waited on a normal headway H. But a longer gap also catches proportionally more people, because passengers keep arriving the whole time the bus is missing. So the total harm scales with G × (G − H) / 2 — roughly the square of the gap. Doubling a gap does not double the damage, it roughly quadruples it. That is why I stopped counting incidents.

What I found

Counting incidents points at different routes than measuring waiting

6 of 28 routes move more than five places when I rank by rider impact instead of by how many incidents they log, and the largest single shift is 10 places. A route with many brief incidents can be almost unnoticeable to a rider; a route with fewer, longer gaps is the one people remember. That gap between the two rankings is the whole reason I built the impact measure.

External causes the largest share of the waiting

Responsibility sits with weather, traffic and events - outside ttc control. It is 23% of logged incidents but 37% of rider impact (+14.4 points), because its incidents run to an average gap of 29 minutes.

Most of the waiting comes from causes the TTC does not control

Weather, traffic and onboard incidents account for 40% of rider impact, against 40% from mechanical and operator causes. That is an uncomfortable finding for anyone hoping for a simple fix, and it argues for building slack into the schedule rather than trying to eliminate the incidents themselves.

Winter costs riders 47% more waiting per day

December through February averages 41,391 on the impact index per day against 28,155 across the rest of the year. The cold stop and the late bus turn out to be the same problem, which is roughly what I expected to find and the reason I started here.
